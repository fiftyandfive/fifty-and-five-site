01

Swallowing and Dysphagia

Dysphagia is difficulty moving food, liquid, or pills safely from the mouth to the stomach. It is common after stroke and with Parkinson's, dementia, and general weakness, and it raises the risk of choking, dehydration, weight loss, and aspiration pneumonia.

SIGNS IT MAY HELP

  • Coughing, throat clearing, or a wet, gurgly voice during or after meals
  • Meals taking much longer, or food left pocketed in the cheeks
  • Unplanned weight loss, or refusing foods and drinks they used to enjoy
  • Pneumonia or chest infections that keep coming back

WHAT A SESSION LOOKS LIKE

We watch a real meal or snack, check the strength and coordination of the mouth and throat, and try strategies on the spot: posture, pacing, bite size, and texture changes using IDDSI diet levels. Treatment adds targeted swallowing exercises. When a closer look is needed, we recommend an instrumental study (modified barium swallow or FEES) through the physician. Whitney is VitalStim certified, so when it fits the plan of care, VitalStim, a form of neuromuscular electrical stimulation, can be added alongside swallowing exercises.

02

Stroke and Aphasia Recovery

Aphasia is a language disorder, most often caused by a stroke. Intelligence is intact, but finding words, following conversation, reading, or writing becomes hard. Therapy rebuilds these skills and gives your parent reliable ways to be understood while recovery continues.

SIGNS IT MAY HELP

  • Knowing what they want to say but not being able to find the word
  • Saying the wrong word, or sentences that do not make sense
  • Trouble following conversation, television, or instructions
  • New difficulty reading mail or writing a simple note

WHAT A SESSION LOOKS LIKE

Structured word finding and comprehension practice built around your parent's own life: family names, favorite topics, the phone, the calendar. We set up supports such as picture boards, written choices, or tablet apps, and show family members how to give cues that help instead of finishing sentences for them.

03

Brain Injury (TBI) Recovery

A concussion or traumatic brain injury can change how a person communicates, thinks, and swallows. Attention, memory, organization, word finding, speech clarity, and safe eating can all be affected, even when the injury looks mild from the outside. Therapy targets the changes that matter most in daily life.

SIGNS IT MAY HELP

  • Trouble concentrating or following conversation
  • Word finding problems, or losing the thread mid sentence
  • Memory and organization problems with plans, appointments, or tasks
  • Slurred speech or swallowing changes after the injury

WHAT A SESSION LOOKS LIKE

Cognitive communication strategies for attention, memory, and organization, practiced inside real life routines: the calendar, the phone, conversations, and household tasks. Goals can include a return to work or school. Family members learn how to support the strategies at home, and speech or swallowing changes are treated alongside when present.

04

Cognitive Communication

Changes in memory, attention, reasoning, and organization affect how a person communicates and manages the day. We work with people living with dementia, mild cognitive impairment, or brain injury to keep them independent and engaged for as long as possible.

SIGNS IT MAY HELP

  • Repeating questions or losing track of conversations
  • Missing medications or appointments that used to be routine
  • Difficulty with bills, the phone, or following a recipe
  • Frustration, withdrawal, or avoiding social situations

WHAT A SESSION LOOKS LIKE

Standardized cognitive testing sets a baseline. Therapy then focuses on practical routines: memory books, medication and calendar systems, and simplified steps for daily tasks. Family members and staff learn communication approaches that lower frustration on both sides.

05

Parkinson's and Voice

Parkinson's often makes the voice softer, breathier, and flatter, and speech can become rushed or mumbled. Many people do not notice how quiet they have become. Voice therapy recalibrates effort, working toward a stronger, clearer voice.

SIGNS IT MAY HELP

  • Family saying “what?” often, or your parent being left out of conversation
  • A voice that is soft, hoarse, or fades at the end of sentences
  • Fast, blurred speech, or trouble getting words started
  • Changes in swallowing alongside the voice changes

WHAT A SESSION LOOKS LIKE

High effort vocal exercises, loudness and pitch practice, and the phrases your parent uses every day, repeated until the new voice carries into real conversation. Voice programs are usually more frequent than other therapy for a set number of weeks.

06

Motor Speech

Dysarthria and apraxia of speech affect the muscles and the planning behind speech after stroke, brain injury, ALS, multiple sclerosis, and other neurological conditions. Your parent knows exactly what to say, but the words come out slurred, slow, or effortful.

SIGNS IT MAY HELP

  • Slurred or mumbled speech that others struggle to understand
  • Groping for the right mouth position, or errors that change each time
  • Running out of breath while talking
  • Speech that gets harder as the day goes on

WHAT A SESSION LOOKS LIKE

Exercises for breath support, articulation, and speaking rate, practiced with the words and phrases your parent uses most. When speech is severely affected, we introduce other ways to communicate, from simple letter boards to speech generating devices.

07

Caregiver Training

The people who help at every meal and every conversation have the biggest effect on safety and progress. Caregiver training gives family members, private aides, and facility staff the exact techniques in the plan of care, practiced hands on until they feel confident.

SIGNS IT MAY HELP

  • You are unsure which foods or drink thicknesses are safe
  • Mealtimes feel tense, rushed, or frightening
  • Conversations often end in frustration for everyone
  • New aides or family members are joining the care team

WHAT A SESSION LOOKS LIKE

We coach during a real meal or daily routine: positioning, pacing, cueing, and what to do if coughing starts. You receive written instructions in plain language, and several caregivers can join the same session. Private pay caregiver training is $150 per session.

02Service line two

School Age

01

Articulation and Speech Sounds

A speech sound disorder makes a child harder to understand. Some sounds are left out, swapped for others, or distorted past the age when most children say them clearly.

SIGNS IT MAY HELP

  • Teachers, friends, or relatives often ask your child to repeat themselves
  • Sounds like r, s, l, or th still come out differently than expected for their age
  • Your child avoids talking in class or gets frustrated when not understood
  • A teacher or pediatrician has mentioned speech clarity

WHAT A SESSION LOOKS LIKE

Games and practice built around the target sounds, moving from single words to sentences to conversation. Parents get a short home practice plan so the new sounds carry over into everyday talk.

02

Expressive and Receptive Language

Receptive language is understanding what others say. Expressive language is putting thoughts into words and sentences. Difficulty with either can affect learning, friendships, and confidence.

SIGNS IT MAY HELP

  • Trouble following directions with more than one step
  • Short or disorganized sentences, or grammar errors that persist
  • Difficulty finding words or explaining an idea
  • Struggling to answer questions about something they just heard

WHAT A SESSION LOOKS LIKE

Structured activities for vocabulary, sentence building, following directions, and answering questions, often using your child's own schoolwork and interests. Parents learn simple ways to build language into the daily routine.

03

Language for Reading and Writing

Reading and writing are built on spoken language. Literacy based language therapy works on the skills underneath: the sounds in words, vocabulary, sentence structure, and understanding and retelling what was read.

SIGNS IT MAY HELP

  • Trouble rhyming or breaking words into their sounds
  • Reading the words but not understanding what was read
  • Written work that is much weaker than your child's spoken ideas
  • Difficulty retelling a story or summarizing a lesson

WHAT A SESSION LOOKS LIKE

Sound awareness, vocabulary, and sentence work tied to real books and class assignments, with practice retelling and writing about what was read. We are glad to coordinate with your child's teacher when you agree.

04

Social Communication

Social communication is how we use language with other people: joining a conversation, taking turns, staying on topic, and reading tone and body language. These unwritten rules come harder to some children.

SIGNS IT MAY HELP

  • Difficulty joining conversations or taking turns in them
  • Missing jokes, sarcasm, or tone of voice
  • Staying on one favorite topic, or changing topics suddenly
  • Misunderstandings that make friendships hard to start or keep

WHAT A SESSION LOOKS LIKE

Practice with real conversation, role play, and everyday social situations your child cares about, broken into clear steps. Parents learn how to coach the same skills at home and with friends.

05

Fluency

Stuttering interrupts the flow of speech with repeated sounds, stretched sounds, or moments of getting stuck. Therapy helps children speak with more ease and feel confident saying what they want to say.

SIGNS IT MAY HELP

  • Repeating sounds or words, stretching sounds, or getting stuck
  • Visible tension, blinking, or extra effort while speaking
  • Avoiding certain words, speaking situations, or answering in class
  • Worry or embarrassment about talking

WHAT A SESSION LOOKS LIKE

Strategies for easier, smoother speech, practiced from short phrases up to conversation, alongside work on attitudes and confidence. Parents learn how to respond in ways that support their child.

06

Concussion and Brain Injury, Return to Learn

After a concussion or brain injury, a child may look fine but find school suddenly harder. Cognitive communication support works on attention, memory, organization, and language, and we coordinate with the family and school on a return to learn plan.

SIGNS IT MAY HELP

  • Trouble paying attention in class or finishing work since the injury
  • Forgetting assignments, directions, or what was just read
  • Difficulty finding words or organizing thoughts when speaking or writing
  • Fatigue, frustration, or falling behind after returning to school

WHAT A SESSION LOOKS LIKE

Practical strategies for attention, memory, and organization, practiced with your child's real schoolwork and routines. With your permission, we share recommendations with teachers and the school team so supports in class match the plan, and parents learn how to help at home.

05Questions families ask

Straight answers before you call.

Do we need a doctor's order?

For Medicare, yes. Medicare requires a plan of care certified by your parent's doctor, nurse practitioner, or physician assistant. We can send the request to your parent's doctor for you. For private pay, an order is not required, though we still share our findings with the physician when you agree.

Does Medicare cover speech therapy at home?

Medicare Part B covers outpatient speech therapy when it is medically necessary, including visits at home or in assisted living. Your parent does not need to be homebound. The usual Part B deductible and coinsurance apply. [ENROLLMENT PENDING]

How long will therapy take?

It depends on the goals, not a fixed program. The plan of care states how often we will visit and for how long, and we review progress with you at least every ten visits. When goals are met, we step down or finish with a home program.

Can I be there, or join from out of town?

Yes, and we encourage it. Family members can sit in on sessions, and we can include you by phone or video. After each visit, you can expect a short update on what we worked on and what to practice.

Mom lives in assisted living. Can you see her there?

Yes. We see residents in their apartment or a quiet space in the community and coordinate with the nursing and wellness staff so everyone follows the same recommendations.

What if Dad is already getting home health?

While a home health agency has an open episode of care, therapy is usually provided through that agency. We can begin when the episode ends, and we are glad to talk with the agency's team about a smooth handoff.

What if my parent does not want therapy?

That is common, and it is worth a conversation. We start with what matters to them, such as enjoying a favorite meal safely or talking with grandchildren on the phone, and build therapy around it.

Do you see children?

Yes. School age children, about ages 5 to 18, are one of the practice's two focus areas. Whitney works with kids on speech sounds, expressive and receptive language, language for reading and writing, social communication, and stuttering.

Can we use the Step Up scholarship?

We are applying to become an approved provider for the Step Up For Students Unique Abilities scholarship [PROVIDER APPROVAL PENDING]. Once approved, eligible families can use their scholarship for evaluations and therapy. Families can also pay privately or ask for a superbill to submit to their own insurance.

Where do sessions happen?

Older adults are seen at home, in assisted living, in memory care, or in skilled nursing. School age children are seen at home after school.

What areas do you serve?

Lake Nona, Orlando, Winter Park, Kissimmee, St. Cloud, Celebration, Dr. Phillips, Windermere, Oviedo, and Lake Mary. If you are nearby but outside these areas, ask us.

02

Swallowing and VitalStim

What are the signs of a swallowing problem in older adults?

Common signs include coughing or throat clearing during or after meals, a wet or gurgly voice after drinking, food that feels stuck, meals that take much longer than they used to, unplanned weight loss, and repeated chest infections. If you notice these, ask the physician about a swallowing evaluation with a speech language pathologist.

What is VitalStim therapy?

VitalStim is a form of neuromuscular electrical stimulation used alongside swallowing exercises. Small electrodes on the neck deliver gentle stimulation while the patient practices swallowing. Whitney is VitalStim certified, and she decides with each patient's physician whether it is appropriate as part of a treatment plan.

Can a speech therapist help with swallowing after a stroke?

Yes. Swallowing difficulty, called dysphagia, is common after a stroke, and a speech language pathologist evaluates how safely a person swallows, recommends the right food and drink textures, and teaches exercises and strategies. Whitney's flagship adult specialty is swallowing.

What are IDDSI diet levels?

IDDSI is the International Dysphagia Diet Standardisation Initiative, a shared scale for how thick drinks should be and how soft or finely textured foods should be for people with swallowing difficulty. Using one scale helps families, kitchens, and care staff prepare meals the same way.

03

Brain injury and concussion

Can speech therapy help after a traumatic brain injury?

Yes. After a traumatic brain injury, a speech language pathologist works on attention, memory, organization, word finding, social communication, and swallowing, depending on what the injury affected. Traumatic brain injury is one of Whitney's two specialties, for adults and for school age children.

What is cognitive communication therapy?

Cognitive communication therapy targets the thinking skills that support communication, such as attention, memory, planning, and problem solving. It helps people after a brain injury, a stroke, or with a neurological condition manage daily conversations, appointments, and tasks more independently.

How does speech therapy help a child return to school after a concussion?

A speech language pathologist can assess attention, processing speed, memory, and language after a concussion, then work with the family and the school on a return to learn plan. That may include temporary classroom supports while the student recovers.

04

School age children

Does my child need speech therapy?

Consider an evaluation if your school age child is hard to understand, has trouble following directions or telling a story in order, struggles with reading and writing because of language, stutters, or finds conversations with peers difficult. An evaluation answers the question clearly and gives you a written plan either way.

What ages do you see?

The practice sees school age children, about ages 5 to 18, and adults of any age, with a focus on older adults. School age sessions cover speech sounds, expressive and receptive language, language for reading and writing, social communication, and stuttering.

Where do children's sessions happen?

School age children are seen at home after school, so families skip the drive. Parents are welcome in sessions and leave with clear activities to practice at home.

Do you work with my child's school or IEP team?

Yes, with your written permission. Whitney can review an IEP or 504 plan, share evaluation results with the school, and coordinate goals so private therapy and school support reinforce each other.

06

Getting started

Do I need a doctor's referral for speech therapy?

Not for private pay. For Medicare, a physician, nurse practitioner, or physician assistant must certify the plan of care, and the practice can send that request to the doctor for you. Either way, findings are shared with the physician when you agree.

What happens at a speech therapy evaluation?

At an evaluation, the therapist reviews history, observes and tests speech, language, thinking, or swallowing depending on the concern, and talks through what matters most to the patient and family. You receive a written report with results, recommendations, and a plan.

How long does speech therapy take?

It depends on the goals, not a fixed program. The plan of care states how often visits happen and for how long, and progress is reviewed with the family regularly. When goals are met, therapy steps down or ends with a home program.

How do I get started?

Request a consultation by phone or through the website. After a short call to understand the situation, you can complete the new patient intake online and schedule the evaluation.
